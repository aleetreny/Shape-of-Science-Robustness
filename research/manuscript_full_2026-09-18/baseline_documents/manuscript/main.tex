\section{Introduction}
Science maps help readers inspect relationships across a body of literature. They can bring neighbouring research areas into view, help locate related papers and support comparisons between fields. With text embeddings, these relationships begin with a model that converts each paper's text into a vector, allowing papers to be compared through distances in a numerical space. Such representations have been used to map biomedical literature and examine the disciplinary structure of physics \citep{refbe130e2973,refc0e2cc29e0}. But the model is part of how the map is made. How far would its conclusions change if the same papers were represented by another model?

The broader problem is familiar in science mapping. \citet{refa0d2d707e6} asked how much a reconstructed thematic structure reflects scientific knowledge and how much it reflects the method used to identify it. In a comparison of text, citation and hybrid relations, \citet{ref6a2f5912bc} found substantial differences in cluster membership even when aggregate accuracy scores were similar. This matters when a map is used to support a specific claim: a summary of overall agreement may say little about whether two methods place the same papers together.

The present comparison follows that problem through a fixed corpus of 500,000 English-language publications from OpenAlex, published in 2000--2024 and assigned to 26 broad Fields. Ten embedding models represent the same papers. The analyses examine relations between field centres, organisation within fields and the neighbours of individual articles. Separate matched subsets test changes to the input text and to how a model's output is reduced to one vector per paper. A subsequent geometric analysis asks whether comparisons between fields retain their direction when both the model and the selected articles change. The aim is to identify which claims about the map survive these changes.

The results show shared broad relationships alongside changes in local neighbours and geometric comparisons between fields. Some models repeatedly place one field above another on a geometric measure, while other models repeatedly give the opposite order. The size of these differences matters, as does their sensitivity to processing choices. Repeating a comparison with different articles therefore addresses only part of its reliability. Agreement across representations provides useful evidence about what is shared, but does not establish which representation best captures the subject matter.

\section{Background and research questions}
\subsection{Representation choices in science mapping}
Comparisons of document embeddings already address several parts of this problem. \citet{refc0e2cc29e0} evaluated text and graph representations against the hierarchical classification of physics papers, including representations based on titles and abstracts. In biomedicine, \citet{refbe130e2973} compared models and ways of reducing their outputs before selecting a representation for a large literature map. Their evaluation used the extent to which nearby papers shared field labels. Both studies make clear that a representation has to be assessed against a stated purpose and criterion.

Even within one model, the document representation is not fully specified by its name. Titles and abstracts supply different information. Models also differ in how much text they accept, while some require a choice of how to combine the representations of individual tokens into a single vector. This operation, usually called pooling, can use an average or a designated position in the output. \citet{singh-singh-2022-inefficiency} examined how scientific language models respond to short queries and text perturbations, showing that changes in wording can alter retrieval behaviour. These choices belong in a comparison of the maps produced by the models.

There are also direct precedents for comparing the geometry of embeddings and their retrieved neighbours together. \citet{ref4e1e0d5582} used both types of comparison across retrieval datasets. \citet{ref6da178065c} examined local density and asymmetry across several document embeddings and disciplines in relation to citation rates. The present study builds on these comparisons by asking which broad, local and geometric conclusions persist across a fixed model panel, with matched articles and explicit controls for text, representation settings and the papers available as neighbours.

\subsection{Agreement, stability and interpretation}
Agreement and stability answer different questions here. Agreement concerns what two representations share when applied to the same articles. Stability concerns whether a specified result persists under a specified change, such as selecting different articles from the corpus. A field ordering may be stable within each model and still disagree between models. Thematic validity requires further evidence about how the represented relationships correspond to the research being described. In their study of invasion biology, \citet{QSSreviewQ17} showed why interpreting algorithmic groupings requires attention to the different relationships between papers that a mapping method can combine. Neither repetition nor model consensus supplies that interpretation on its own.

The term \emph{shape} also needs an explicit object. Here it covers relationships in the embedding spaces, before any projection into two dimensions. Comparing the centres of fields describes a broad organisation; comparing articles within a field examines its internal structure. Neighbour agreement asks whether the same papers remain closest to a given article. Two further properties describe the angular spread of a field's vectors and their effective linear dimension, which summarises how variation is distributed across directions. A field with greater geometric spread is not thereby more diverse in its topics, and effective linear dimension does not count those topics. The comparisons below retain these distinctions, so that a change in one property is not treated as evidence that every part of the map has changed.

\subsection{Research questions and scope}
Three questions organise the analysis:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{RQ1.}] Which structural relationships are shared across embedding models, and how does agreement vary between field centres, articles within fields and local neighbours?
\item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How much do text input and pooling choices change geometry and neighbours relative to changing the embedding model?
\item[\textbf{RQ3.}] Which geometric comparisons between fields persist, reverse or remain unresolved across models and article selections, and how large are the differences?
\end{enumerate}

These are descriptive questions about the selected corpus and models. The third question arose in an exploratory extension after the initial comparisons; the sequence of checks is documented in the methods and supplement. OpenAlex labels organise the comparisons and are retained with their known errors. They are not an independent reference for thematic correctness. The study consequently evaluates the persistence of conclusions within this design, without ranking models by their ability to recover a single correct map of science.
