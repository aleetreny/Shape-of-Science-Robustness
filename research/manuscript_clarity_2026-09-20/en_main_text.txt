How much does the map of science depend on
the embedding model?
Alejandro Treny Ortega
Independent researcher
Manuscript draft • 20 September 2026
Abstract
Science maps use relationships between papers to describe how research is organised.
When these relationships come from text embeddings, a model turns each paper into
a list of numbers. Would another model give the same picture? I compare ten models
using the same collection of 500,000 OpenAlex records, covering 26 research ﬁelds and
2000–2024. I examine the overall arrangement, the papers placed closest to each other,
and the spread of papers within ﬁelds. The models share a broad arrangement, but in
searches within the same ﬁeld and period, two models retain about eight of the same
25 closest papers on average. Using only the title instead of the title and abstract
changes these neighbours about as much as changing models. In a later exploratory
comparison, models also disagree about which of two ﬁelds has more widely spread
paper representations. These opposite results can persist when diﬀerent articles are
selected, although many diﬀerences are small and processing choices matter. Repeating
a result with other articles therefore does not ensure that another model supports it.
The ﬁndings identify dependence on representation; they do not establish which model
best describes the research itself.
Keywords: science mapping; document embeddings; robustness; neighbourhood agreement;
geometric comparisons.
1 Introduction
A science map can help a reader ﬁnd related papers or compare research areas. Its usefulness
depends on the relationships it shows. If two models place diﬀerent papers close to the same
article, the reader may follow diﬀerent routes through the literature. If they disagree about
how widely an area’s papers are spread, they may also support diﬀerent descriptions of
that area. I ask how much of this changes when the papers stay the same and only their
representation changes.
Text embeddings provide one way to build these maps. A model reads a paper’s text and
turns it into a list of numbers, called a vector. Those numbers give the paper a position
in a space where distances can be calculated. Papers placed close together are treated as

related. This approach has been used to map biomedical literature and the structure of
physics ( González-Márquez et al. , 2024; Constantino et al. , 2025). But the position comes
from the model. It is not a relationship observed independently of that model.
This concern has a history in science mapping. Gläser et al. (2017) asked how much a
reconstructed thematic structure reﬂects scientiﬁc knowledge and how much it reﬂects the
method used to recover it. Boyack and Klavans (2020) found that methods could obtain
similar overall accuracy while assigning diﬀerent papers to clusters. An overall score can
therefore leave an important question unanswered: would another method support the par-
ticular comparison a reader wants to make?
I address that question using ten models and a ﬁxed collection of 500,000 publications. First,
I compare the broad arrangement of research areas and the articles placed near each other.
I then ask whether changing the text supplied to a model matters as much as changing
the model itself. A later, exploratory analysis examines speciﬁc comparisons between areas:
does each model identify the same area as having more widely spread paper representations?
Repeating these comparisons with other articles helps distinguish dependence on the selected
papers from dependence on the model.
2 Background and research questions
2.1 What earlier comparisons tell us
Previous studies assess scientiﬁc embeddings against several useful criteria. Constantino
et al. (2025) compare them with disciplinary classiﬁcations in physics; González-Márquez
et al. (2024) examine related biomedical papers and their labels. Such checks ask whether
a representation agrees with information about the literature. The present study asks a
complementary question: which relationships remain when the representation changes?
The model is only one choice involved. A title and a full abstract contain diﬀerent amounts of
information. Models also read diﬀerent lengths of text and combine their outputs in diﬀerent
ways. Singh and Singh (2022) show that scholarly search can be sensitive to the wording of
short queries. This makes the text supplied to a model part of the comparison, rather than
a detail to leave unspeciﬁed.
Comparing both overall structure and nearby papers is not new. Caspari et al. (2024)
combine geometric and neighbourhood comparisons, while Imel and Hafen (2025) examine
properties of scientiﬁc embedding spaces alongside citation relationships. I build on these ap-
proaches by keeping articles matched between models, changing text and processing choices
explicitly, and checking speciﬁc comparisons between research areas with repeated article
selections and alternative measures.

2.2 What agreement can establish
Two distinctions matter throughout. Agreement asks whether diﬀerent models give similar
results for the same papers. Selection stability asks whether a result repeats when diﬀerent
papers from the collection are used. A comparison can be stable within each model while
the models give opposite answers.
Neither property establishes that the answer is correct about the research itself. Models can
share an error, and a repeatable pattern can be hard to interpret. In a study of invasion
biology, Held and Velden (2022) show why topic solutions require substantive interpretation
beyond numerical checks. Here, agreement is evidence that a result survives a change of
model, not evidence of thematic truth.
I compare the numerical representations before they are reduced to a drawing on a page.
Each vector has hundreds of coordinates; a two-dimensional plot would add another trans-
formation that could change apparent distances. In this article, the shape of a map refers
to relationships in that original space.
2.3 Research questions
RQ1. When the model changes, what remains of the arrangement of research areas and of
the papers placed close together?
RQ2. How much does changing the input text, or the rule used to form one paper vector,
matter compared with changing the model?
RQ3. Do models agree about which research area has more widely spread paper repre-
sentations, or variation across more directions? Do these comparisons repeat with other
articles?
The third question was added after the initial comparisons and is exploratory. All three
concern a ﬁxed collection and a ﬁxed set of models. They do not seek a winning model or
estimate the structure of all scientiﬁc literature.

3 Data and methods
3.1 One collection, several questions
The starting collection contains 500,000 distinct OpenAlex work IDs, retrieved and cleaned
by 15 September 2026 ( Priem et al. , 2022). Records were labelled as English articles, reviews
or conference papers published in 2000–2024. Each required a title, a reconstructed abstract
of at least 50 words and a primary research ﬁeld. Retractions, paratext and clear content
failures were excluded. Less certain cases and possible duplicate versions were kept with
ﬂags, rather than treated as resolved. Supplement S1 gives the rules and counts.
OpenAlex assigns papers to broad Fields and narrower Subﬁelds, using information from
text, citations and journals ( OpenAlex, 2026). I refer to these as areas and specialties,
respectively. The 26 areas provide the main units of comparison. These database labels
organise the analysis; they are not independently veriﬁed descriptions of every paper.
The collection has two parts. Seeded random API requests supplied 400,000 eligible records.
Another 100,000 strengthened coverage where few papers were available within an area and
ﬁve-year period. The ﬁve periods are 2000–04, 2005–09, 2010–14, 2015–19 and 2020–24,
giving 130 area–period groups with at least 2,165 papers each. Global searches use the
400,000-record general collection. Area comparisons use both parts. Neither the reinforced
collection nor an average giving every area equal weight estimates world publication propor-
tions.
Not every question requires processing all 500,000 papers again. To compare titles, abstracts
and both together, I use the same 52,000 papers: 2,000 per area, with 400 from each period.
This includes the 26,000 papers used in an earlier, smaller trial of that same comparison. The
later analysis of spread within areas reuses these 52,000 papers. A separate set of 52,000
serves a diﬀerent purpose: checking what happens when every model reads the same passage
of text. Table 1 keeps these uses apart. In every model comparison, both models receive the
same article IDs.

3.2 How each model represents a paper
The ten models in Table 2 cover scientiﬁc documents, general sentence similarity, and scien-
tiﬁc or biomedical language. They were selected for their use in scholarly representation and
their diﬀerent training approaches. They are a ﬁxed comparison set, not a random sample
of models or a ranking of the newest systems. Model versions, text formats and software
settings were ﬁxed before their representations were calculated.
The main input is the title followed by the abstract, in each model’s usual format. Models
read small pieces of text called tokens, which may be whole words or parts of words. Their
input limits therefore diﬀer from word counts. MiniLM reads at most 256 tokens and MPNet
384 in their standard settings. These limits cut oﬀ part of the input for 49.58% and 20.16% of

papers, respectively. I retain those settings and test MiniLM at 512 tokens separately. The
common-passage check described above lets every model read the same content, although
each divides it into tokens in its own way.
Some models produce a vector for each token, so a further rule must turn those outputs into
one vector for the paper. This step is called pooling. For BERT, SciBERT, BioBERT and
PubMedBERT, the main rule averages the last-layer outputs at all positions that contain
text or special symbols, excluding padding. These four models were not trained speciﬁcally
to compare whole documents. Two checks instead use the ﬁrst-position output, called CLS,
or the last non-padding output, called SEP. CLS is taken before the model’s additional pooler
transformation. The mean rule was speciﬁed before the primary comparisons and retained
after early checks; it was not chosen to maximise agreement. The other six models use their
documented rules. Supplement S1 provides exact settings.

3.3 Following the map from areas to individual papers
Before comparing representations, each article vector is scaled to length one. This makes
comparisons depend on direction rather than vector length. To summarise an area, I average
its article vectors to obtain a centre. The arrangement of these centres describes broad
relationships between areas. Comparisons of the individual vectors inside each area retain
the detail hidden by that average.
The centre comparison uses 256 articles per centre, with 26 area centres, 217 specialty centres,
or a restricted set of 26 specialty centres. Random groups with the same sizes and dates show
how much agreement can accompany averaging without the observed labels. Within-group
comparisons use 128, 256 and 512 articles. The same 183 specialties have enough papers for
all three sizes.
For an individual paper, its neighbours are the papers placed closest to it. I compare the
lists of 25 nearest neighbours produced by two models, with lists of 10 and 50 as checks.
Closeness is measured by cosine similarity, which compares vector directions. The starting
paper cannot be its own neighbour. Search lists are calculated exactly and checked by
independent sorting. The available papers matter: a search through an entire area and
period diﬀers from one limited to 2,048 papers or extended to the 400,000-record general
collection. Each result states which search was used.
A further comparison holds the search size constant while changing its scope. For each of 217
specialties, 50 ﬁxed starting papers are searched against 256 candidates from that specialty
and 256 from its broader area. Both sets include those same 50 papers and have matching
period counts. Ten selections vary the other 206 candidates. This gives 10,850 starting
papers in total. Including them in both sets helps keep the comparison fair, but also means
that the broader set is not an unrestricted random sample of its area.
3.4 Measuring shared structure and spread within an area
To compare overall structure, I ask whether relationships among the same papers are similar
in two representations. The main measure is corrected linear centred kernel alignment,
abbreviated CKA (Kornblith et al., 2019; Murphy et al., 2024). Higher values indicate greater
agreement. A value of 0.9 does not mean that 90% of the science is correctly represented.
The correction reduces a known inﬂation caused by ﬁnite samples; it does not remove all
statistical bias. Two further checks allow a rotation and rescaling of the representations
(Procrustes alignment), or compare the ranking of distances between papers. They have
diﬀerent sensitivities, but are not independent tests of thematic correctness ( Williams et al. ,
2021; Williams, 2024). Supplement S2 gives the formulas.
I also describe two properties within each area. Angular spread measures how far apart article
vectors point: it is the median angle between distinct vectors. Larger angles mean a wider

spread of directions. Eﬀective linear dimension , measured by the participation ratio (PR),
asks how widely variation is shared among directions. For intuition, a cloud concentrated
along one line varies mainly in one direction; a cloud spread across a plane varies in more.
The measure extends that idea to the full vector space. Neither property counts research
topics or establishes how diverse an area is.
Alternative angle summaries and measures of variation test dependence on the chosen for-
mula. The dimension alternatives use the same underlying variation, so they do not indepen-
dently validate a thematic interpretation. I also tested whether links between nearby papers
could identify separate groups, but checks with artiﬁcial examples prevented treating that
measure as general fragmentation. Deﬁnitions and controls appear in Supplement S6.
3.5 Checking whether a comparison repeats
With 26 areas, there are 325 ways to choose two diﬀerent areas, counting each comparison
once. For each geometric property, I ask which area has the larger value under each model.
I repeat the calculation using twenty half samples of 1,000 papers per area and ﬁve further
selections of 2,000. A direction is called persistent only if it holds in all these selections and
in the main set of 2,000.
This separates three outcomes. All ten models may consistently identify the same area as
larger. At least two ﬁxed models may consistently give opposite answers. Or the comparison
may remain unresolved under these rules. Unresolved does not mean equal. I also require
diﬀerences to exceed 1, 5 or 10% of the two values’ average, alongside the check that requires
only a nonzero diﬀerence. These cutoﬀs show whether very small diﬀerences drive a result;
they are not universal thresholds of scientiﬁc importance.
Selections overlap within the same collection. Their ranges describe sensitivity to choosing ar-
ticles, not conﬁdence intervals for a wider population. Separate checks ﬂag comparisons that
change too much across selections; the supplement retains their rules and counts. Compar-
isons also share models and areas, so they are described rather than treated as independent
observations in signiﬁcance tests.
The work developed in stages. The ﬁrst structure and neighbour comparisons preceded the
expanded text experiment and the specialty searches. The geometric analysis was a later
exploration; the 325-comparison summary followed its initial examples. Rules were recorded
before the corresponding new calculations, with earlier results already known. Some controls
were added during this process. Supplement S2 records the sequence; the study was not
externally preregistered as a whole.

The alternative measures broadly support the ordering of model agreements. Across the

4.2 Changing the model changes which papers appear nearby
Within the same area and period, two models retain an average of 30.3% of the 25 nearest
neighbours: about eight papers out of 25. This average gives equal weight to each group and
model pair. Using 2,048 common candidates in every group raises agreement to 31.9%. A
separate search through the 400,000-record general collection gives 17.5% for 13,000 balanced
starting papers. That last comparison changes both the search scope and the number of
candidates, so it cannot isolate the eﬀect of crossing disciplinary boundaries.
Holding search size and dates constant gives a less uniform result (Figure 2). Of 217 special-
ties, 127 have lower agreement when searching within the specialty rather than the broader
area, while 90 have higher agreement. The mean change is −1.35 percentage points. With
50 neighbours, the split is 109 and 108. A narrower search therefore does not consistently
make models disagree more.
Overall structure and neighbour agreement are related: their rank correlation across the
45 model-pair averages is 0.920. In other words, model pairs that agree more on one tend
to agree more on the other. Yet a pair can preserve broad structure while changing many
individual neighbours. These shared-model observations are descriptive, not independent
evidence of reliability.
The case analysis in Supplement S4 follows individual specialties, papers and links across
changes in sample size, pooling and neighbour count. It also exposes source problems: se-
lected examples include a history book labelled as physics, a bacterial-infection text labelled
as politics and an announcement. These match the downloaded OpenAlex records; they are
not errors in aligning model outputs. They remain in the results and do not establish how
common such problems are.

4.4 A comparison between two areas can reverse under another
model
The geometric analysis reuses the 52,000-paper text sample and its title-plus-abstract repre-
sentations. It asks, for example, whether one area’s paper vectors point further apart than
another’s. Before comparing areas, I checked whether each property’s value repeated across
article selections. Angular spread has no alerts among the 260 model–area cases. Eﬀective
linear dimension has four alerts in the half-sample check and none in the ﬁve equal-size
selections. The latter result does not erase the former.
Across the 325 comparisons between two areas, 262 have persistent opposite answers from
at least two models for angular spread; 221 do for eﬀective linear dimension (Figure 4). For
example, one ﬁxed model consistently places area A above B, while another consistently
places B above A. This does not mean that all ten models disagree. All ten give the same
persistent direction in 42 and 54 comparisons, respectively; the remaining 21 and 50 are
unresolved.
Many opposite answers involve small diﬀerences. Requiring a diﬀerence above 5% of the two
area values’ average leaves 96 opposite comparisons for angular spread and 177 for dimension.
At 10%, the counts fall to 19 and 126. The direction of disagreement and its size therefore
need to be reported together.
Alternative measures retain the same opposing models and their directions in 225 of the 262
angular comparisons and 196 of the 221 dimension comparisons. The angular check covers
all 26 selections; the dimension alternatives cover three. They describe related properties
and do not establish which result is correct about the research. Restricting the comparison
to the six models trained for document or sentence similarity still leaves 231 angular and
160 dimension oppositions at the nonzero cutoﬀ (Tables S14–S15). A smaller model set also
oﬀers fewer chances to ﬁnd opposition.
Processing choices can change the conclusion. A check that subtracts the overall mean vector
and then rescales vectors to length one preserves the same opposing models and directions
in 145 of the 262 angular comparisons and 218 of the 221 dimension comparisons. These
are checks of the saved point estimates, not another complete series of repeated selections.
Pooling changes can also reverse directions (Table S16).
The attempted fragmentation measure gives a useful limit. Artiﬁcial elongated clouds or
extreme points can appear disconnected even without separate thematic groups. Results also
depend on sample size and the rule for linking neighbours; 51 of 260 cases retain selection
alerts. I therefore report those checks in Supplement S6 without interpreting disconnection
as evidence that a research area is fragmented.

5 Discussion
5.1 What should be checked before using a map
The models share a broad arrangement, yet can give diﬀerent answers about nearby papers
or the spread of representations within two areas. This matters when a map supports a
speciﬁc statement. A reassuring overall score does not establish that the statement survives
a change of model. Repeating the article selection addresses a diﬀerent question and cannot
substitute for that check.

The concern follows earlier work on method-dependent science maps ( Gläser et al. , 2017;
Boyack and Klavans, 2020) and complements assessments against external classiﬁcations and
relationships ( González-Márquez et al. , 2024; Constantino et al. , 2025). Combining struc-
tural and neighbour comparisons also has precedent ( Caspari et al. , 2024). The contribution
here is the controlled comparison across levels, text choices and repeated area comparisons,
including cases where a result persists within models but reverses between them.
Interpretation still depends on what the measure describes. A larger angle means that
vectors point further apart. A larger eﬀective linear dimension means that variation is less
concentrated in a few directions. Neither, by itself, shows greater thematic diversity or
interdisciplinarity. Related work on embedding geometry also cautions against assigning a
broad meaning to a convenient measure ( Rudman et al. , 2022). The failed fragmentation
check makes this concrete: a repeatable diﬀerence can still support the wrong explanation.
5.2 What the additional checks change
Text and processing choices belong in reports of science maps. In this collection, removing
the abstract can matter about as much as changing models. A model name alone does not
say what representation was used; the input, text limit and pooling rule are also needed.
The additional discipline and time checks discourage a single explanation for all diﬀerences
(Supplement S5). Medicine has lower structural agreement than Energy under the main rule,
but not always the lowest neighbour agreement. Balancing specialty composition and remov-
ing the two biomedical models do not eliminate the Medicine–Energy diﬀerence. Apparent
model families also change with pooling. With ten models, training data, architecture and
training objectives remain entangled; these associations do not isolate a cause.
Across publication periods, structural agreement increases slightly on average. Neighbour
agreement changes by −0.87 percentage points when all candidates are available but by
+0.91 points with 2,048 candidates in each group. The change of sign shows why search
opportunities matter. Contemporary models applied to older and newer papers do not, on
their own, demonstrate a historical convergence of science.
5.3 What remains open
The collection requires English-labelled records with usable abstracts. Its reinforcement of
smaller area–period groups helps comparison but changes proportions. Source errors remain,
and cleaning is not a certiﬁcation of every record. Repeated selections cannot repair missing
coverage or make these results representative of all scientiﬁc work.
The ten models also set a boundary. Four were not trained speciﬁcally for document simi-
larity. The six-model check helps show that disagreement is not conﬁned to those four, but
does not make the panel representative of all encoders. Deciding which direction is better

Finally, several extensions and controls followed earlier results. Recording their rules before
new calculations improves traceability without turning the whole study into a conﬁrma-
tory design. A useful next step would test a deﬁned map-based claim against independent
judgments in a research area. Adding models alone would not settle what their shared or
conﬂicting geometry means.
6 Conclusion
Changing the embedding model can preserve the broad arrangement of science while changing
the papers found nearby and the direction of comparisons between areas. The text given to a
model can matter just as much. Some results repeat with other articles yet remain dependent
on the model. For a claim drawn from a science map, the relevant check is therefore whether
that claim survives the changes that could aﬀect it, with the size of the diﬀerence made
clear. Agreement identiﬁes what is shared by the tested representations; establishing what
is correct about the research requires further evidence.
