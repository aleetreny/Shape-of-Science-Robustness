Shape of Science Robustness Manuscript draft
How much does the map of science depend on
the embedding model?
Alejandro Treny Ortega
Independent researcher
Manuscript draft • 20 September 2026
Abstract
Science maps describe relationships between papers. Text embedding models build
these relationships by turning a paper’s text into a list of numbers that gives it a
position. Would another model give the same picture? I compare ten models using
titles and abstracts of 500,000 publications from the OpenAlex bibliographic catalogue,
published in 2000–2024 across 26 disciplines, including Medicine and Physics. Repre-
senting each discipline by its papers’ average position produces similar arrangements
across models, including when other papers are selected. Yet within a discipline and
period, two models share, on average, only eight of the 25 papers closest to a given
paper. Supplying titles alone also changes much of that list, with eﬀects that vary
between models. Models can also disagree about which discipline’s papers are more
spread out. When spread is measured through angles from a common reference point,
moving that point can change the comparison even when selecting other papers does
not. These checks distinguish agreement between models from stability to article selec-
tion. The broad arrangement can remain similar while answers about nearby papers
and diﬀerences between disciplines depend on the model, the text it receives and how
its output is processed.
Keywords: science mapping; document embeddings; robustness; neighbourhood agreement;
geometric comparisons.
1 Introduction
A science map can help a reader ﬁnd related papers or compare research areas. Its usefulness
depends on the relationships it shows. When a model determines those relationships from
text, choosing the model becomes part of choosing the map. Two models may lead a reader
to diﬀerent papers near the same starting article, or disagree about how widely an area’s
papers are spread. I ask how much the answer changes when we keep the papers and use
another model to represent them.
The models studied here read a paper’s title and abstract and turn that text into a list of
numbers, called a vector or embedding. The numbers act as coordinates: together they give

the paper a position from which distances to other papers can be calculated. Papers placed
close together are treated as related. This approach has been used to map biomedical litera-
ture and the structure of physics ( González-Márquez et al. , 2024; Constantino et al. , 2025).
But the position comes from the model. It is not a relationship observed independently of
it, and the title and abstract do not contain everything in the paper.
This concern has a history in science mapping. Gläser et al. (2017) asked how much a
reconstructed thematic structure reﬂects scientiﬁc knowledge and how much it reﬂects the
method used to recover it. Boyack and Klavans (2020) found that methods could obtain
similar overall accuracy while assigning diﬀerent papers to clusters. An overall score can
therefore leave an important question unanswered: would another method support the par-
ticular comparison a reader wants to make?
I use ten models and a ﬁxed collection of 500,000 publications to examine three questions.
The ﬁrst concerns shared relationships: do models preserve the broad arrangement of areas
and identify the same nearby papers? The second asks how much of a change comes from
what the model reads: does supplying only a title matter as much as switching models? The
third, added as a later exploration, concerns a description we might draw from the map: do
models agree that one area’s papers are more spread out than another’s? Repeating each
type of comparison with other selected articles also shows whether its answer depends on
the particular papers used.
2 Background and research questions
2.1 What earlier comparisons tell us
Previous studies assess scientiﬁc embeddings against several useful criteria. Constantino
et al. (2025) compare them with disciplinary classiﬁcations in physics; González-Márquez
et al. (2024) examine related biomedical papers and their labels. Such checks ask whether
a representation agrees with information about the literature. The present study asks a
complementary question: which relationships remain when the representation changes?
The model is only one choice involved. The input text is the text we give it to read: a
title alone conveys less information than a title followed by its abstract. Models also accept
diﬀerent lengths of text and use diﬀerent rules to produce a single vector for it. Singh and
Singh (2022) show that scholarly search can be sensitive to the wording of short queries. The
supplied text therefore belongs in the comparison alongside the model.
Comparing both overall structure and nearby papers is not new. Caspari et al. (2024)
combine geometric and neighbourhood comparisons, while Imel and Hafen (2025) examine
properties of scientiﬁc embedding spaces alongside citation relationships. I build on these ap-
proaches by keeping articles matched between models, changing text and processing choices

explicitly, and checking speciﬁc comparisons between research areas with repeated article
selections and alternative measures.
2.2 What agreement can establish
Two changes need to be kept separate. To measure agreement, I keep the articles ﬁxed and
compare models. To check selection stability, I keep the model ﬁxed and choose other articles
under the same rules. An article selection is simply a set chosen from the existing collection;
repeating it does not mean downloading new papers. A result can repeat with other articles
in each model while the models consistently give opposite answers.
Neither property establishes that the answer is correct about the research itself. Models can
share an error, and a repeatable pattern can be hard to interpret. In a study of invasion
biology, Held and Velden (2022) show why topic solutions require substantive interpretation
beyond numerical checks. Here, agreement is evidence that a result survives a change of
model, not evidence of thematic truth.
The word map needs one further distinction here. I compare the numerical positions, each
with hundreds of coordinates, before reducing them to a drawing. A two-dimensional plot
would add a transformation that could change apparent distances. Structure means the pat-
tern of relationships among the objects being compared, whether papers or area summaries.
Spread describes how far apart the representations lie within one area. Two models can share
much of the broad structure without agreeing on every area’s spread.
2.3 Research questions
RQ1. When the model changes, what remains of the arrangement of research areas and of
the papers placed close together?
RQ2. How much does changing the input text, or the rule used to form one paper vector,
matter compared with changing the model?
RQ3. Do models agree about which area’s paper representations are more spread out, or
whether that spread is concentrated along a few directions rather than many? Do these
comparisons repeat with other articles?
The third question was added after the initial comparisons and is exploratory. All three
concern a ﬁxed collection and a ﬁxed set of models. They do not seek a winning model or
estimate the structure of all scientiﬁc literature.

3 Data and methods
3.1 One collection, several questions
OpenAlex is a bibliographic catalogue that supplies titles, abstracts and other information
about publications ( Priem et al. , 2022). The starting collection, or corpus, contains 500,000
distinct work IDs retrieved and cleaned by 15 September 2026. Records were labelled as
English articles, reviews or conference papers published in 2000–2024. Each required a title,
an abstract of at least 50 words reconstructed from OpenAlex’s stored format, and a primary
research area. Retractions, ancillary material and clear content failures were excluded. Less
certain cases and possible duplicate versions were retained with review ﬂags. Those ﬂags
record a concern for later checks; they do not establish that every retained record is correct.
Supplement S1 gives the rules and counts.
OpenAlex assigns papers to broad Fields and narrower Subﬁelds, using information from
text, citations and journals ( OpenAlex, 2026). I call these areas and specialties. For example,
Medicine is an area; Cardiology and Cardiovascular Medicine is one of its specialties. The
26 areas provide the main units of comparison. These database labels organise the analysis;
they are not independently veriﬁed descriptions of every paper.
The collection has two parts. Reproducible random requests to OpenAlex supplied 400,000
eligible records. Another 100,000 were added to strengthen groups that initially had few
papers from the same area and ﬁve-year period. For example, Medicine in 2000–04 is one such
group. The ﬁve periods are 2000–04, 2005–09, 2010–14, 2015–19 and 2020–24. Combined
with the 26 areas, they give 130 area–period groups, each with at least 2,165 papers. Searches
across the whole general collection use the original 400,000 records; comparisons within areas
can use both parts. The reinforcement makes smaller groups easier to compare, but changes
their share of the collection. Neither that share nor an average giving every area equal weight
estimates world publication proportions.
All ten models represent the 500,000 papers using titles and abstracts, but each subsequent
question uses the articles it needs. The input-text experiment compares titles, abstracts
and both together on the same 52,000 papers: 2,000 per area, with 400 from each period.
These include the 26,000 used in the ﬁrst, smaller trial. The later study of spread within
areas reuses this set and its title-plus-abstract vectors. The common-passage check uses a
diﬀerent set of 52,000 papers to ensure that every model reads exactly the same passage,
short enough to ﬁt all input limits. This separates a choice about content from a choice
about length. Table 1 is a guide to these comparisons; its rows overlap and should not be
added together. Within any comparison, the models always represent the same paper IDs.

3.2 How each model represents a paper
The ten models in Table 2 were already trained before this study. I use their published
versions to convert text into vectors, without training them again on this collection. They
cover scientiﬁc documents, general sentence similarity, and scientiﬁc or biomedical language.
Their use in scholarly representation and their diﬀerent training approaches motivated the
selection. This is a ﬁxed comparison set, not a random sample of models or a ranking of the
5

newest systems. Versions, input formats and software settings were ﬁxed before calculating
the vectors.
The main input is the title followed by the abstract, in each model’s usual format. Models
divide text into small pieces called tokens, which can be whole words or parts of words. A
token limit is therefore not a word limit. Under their standard settings, MiniLM reads at
most 256 tokens and MPNet 384; text beyond that limit is cut oﬀ. This aﬀects 49.58% and
20.16% of the papers, respectively. I retain those settings and test MiniLM at 512 tokens
separately. The common-passage check addresses the resulting diﬀerence in what models
can read: all receive the same content, although each splits it into tokens in its own way.
A further choice concerns how a model produces one vector for the whole paper. BERT,
SciBERT, BioBERT and PubMedBERT produce an output vector at each token position,
but were not trained speciﬁcally to compare whole documents. The main rule averages
those outputs after the model has ﬁnished processing the text. This step is called mean
pooling. The average includes special symbols used to mark the text and excludes empty
positions added as padding. Two alternatives take only the output at the ﬁrst special-symbol
position (CLS) or at the ﬁnal non-padding position (SEP). These outputs have processed
the surrounding text; they do not represent only the ﬁrst or last word. The mean rule was
speciﬁed before the primary comparisons and retained after early checks, rather than chosen
to maximise agreement. The other six models use their documented rules. Table 2 and
Supplement S1 specify the exact settings.
3.3 Following the map from areas to individual papers
A vector can be pictured as an arrow from a common starting point to the paper’s position.
Before comparison, I scale every arrow to length one, keeping the direction in which it
points. This operation is called normalisation; it prevents diﬀerences in vector length from
determining closeness. To summarise an area, I average its article vectors and also scale
the resulting vector to length one. I call this summary its centre. Comparing the centres
asks whether models place areas in similar relationships to one another. Comparing the
individual papers inside an area asks how much of its internal organisation they share.
The ﬁrst centre comparison ﬁxes 256 papers per group, so that unequal group sizes do not
drive the comparison. It considers the 26 areas, the 217 specialties, and a restricted set
containing one specialty from each area. To test dependence on which papers were chosen, I
repeat article selection ﬁfty times while keeping each group’s counts from the ﬁve publication
periods. To test dependence on size, I use 128, 256 and 512 papers for the same 26 areas and
the 183 specialties that have enough papers at every size. These are comparison settings,
not a claim that one size is universally suﬃcient.
Each selection also supplies a reference: the same papers are randomly redistributed into
groups while preserving group sizes and publication-period counts. The reference asks how

3.4 Finding and comparing nearby papers
For an individual paper, its neighbours are the papers placed closest to it. The starting paper
is the one whose neighbours we seek; the candidates are all papers available for that search.
Each model returns the closest 25, and agreement is the fraction that appears in both models’
lists. Lists of 10 and 50 provide checks. Closeness uses cosine similarity: arrows pointing
in more similar directions count as closer. The starting paper cannot be its own neighbour.
The lists are calculated exactly and checked by independent sorting.
A search depends on the papers available to it. Searching among every paper from the same
area and period gives diﬀerent groups diﬀerent numbers of candidates. A separate check ﬁxes

2,048 candidates per group to remove that diﬀerence in count. Within a group, every model
searches the same papers. A further search uses the 400,000 general records without area or
period restrictions. These comparisons answer diﬀerent questions, so each result speciﬁes its
candidate set.
The quality check asks whether ﬂagged records and duplicates explain disagreement between
models. Applying the existing review ﬂags and duplicate rule leaves 448,886 records. I
compare neighbour lists for the same retained starting papers before and after ﬁltering the
candidates. Removing records also reduces search size, so further checks hold that size ﬁxed:
1,024 candidates in all 130 groups, and 2,048 in the 63 groups still large enough after ﬁltering.
The before-and-after sets include the same 100 clean starting papers per group. Here, clean
means passing the recorded ﬁlter; it does not mean that every possible source error has been
ruled out.
To separate search scope from search size, I compare a specialty with its parent area using the
same candidate count and matched dates. Consider 50 ﬁxed starting papers from Cardiology
and Cardiovascular Medicine. For each, we search among 256 cardiology papers and among
256 papers from Medicine. Both sets include those same 50 starting papers, leaving 206
places for others; the current starting paper is excluded when its neighbours are ranked. I
choose the other 206 ten times, keeping the 50 ﬁxed and matching publication-period counts
between searches. The procedure covers 217 specialties, giving 10,850 starting papers. It
deliberately includes the 50 specialty papers in the broader set too. Its result therefore
applies to this matched comparison, rather than to an unrestricted random selection from
Medicine.
3.5 Measuring shared structure
To measure shared structure, I compare the pattern of relationships among the same objects
under two models. The objects can be individual papers or the centres that summarise areas.
The main measure is corrected linear centred kernel alignment, abbreviated CKA ( Kornblith
et al., 2019; Murphy et al. , 2024). It compares these patterns without requiring a coordinate
in one model to mean the same thing as a coordinate in another. A value near one, such as
0.9, indicates that the patterns are very similar on this measure. It does not mean that a
corresponding percentage of science is correctly represented. The correction reduces a known
upward distortion associated with ﬁnite samples; it does not remove all statistical bias.
Two further measures check other aspects of shared structure. Procrustes alignment allows
the representations to rotate and change scale before comparing them. The distance-rank
check asks whether pairs of papers have a similar ordering from nearer to further apart.
These measures need not give identical answers ( Williams et al. , 2021; Williams, 2024).
Their formulas appear in Supplement S2.

3.6 Describing spread within an area
A diﬀerent question concerns the shape formed by the papers within each area. I describe two
properties. Angular spread summarises the angles between their vectors, using the median-
distance convention in Supplement S6. Picture arrows drawn from a common point to the
papers: larger angles mean that the arrows point further apart.
Eﬀective linear dimension describes how that cloud of paper positions spreads across direc-
tions. Here, variation means diﬀerences between the papers’ coordinates. A cloud concen-
trated along a line varies mainly in one direction; one spread across a plane varies in more.
The participation ratio, or PR, extends this idea to the full vector space and allows values
between whole numbers. A larger PR means that variation is less concentrated in a few
directions. Neither PR nor angular spread counts research topics or establishes thematic
diversity.
Alternative angle summaries and dimension measures check how much the answer depends
on the formula. For dimension, the entropy-based alternative measures how evenly variation
is distributed. D80 counts the fewest directions needed to account for 80% of that variation.
Because these alternatives describe the same underlying variation, agreement among them
would not independently validate a thematic interpretation. I also tested a further idea:
whether weak links between nearby papers could reveal an area split into separate groups.
Artiﬁcial examples exposed problems with reading that signal as thematic fragmentation.
Supplement S6 gives the deﬁnitions and controls.
3.7 Checking whether a comparison repeats
With 26 areas, there are 325 ways to choose two diﬀerent areas, counting each pair once.
For each property, each model gives a value for area A and a value for area B. I ask whether
the same area keeps the larger value when diﬀerent papers are chosen. The main calculation
uses 2,000 papers per area. I repeat it with twenty half samples of 1,000 and ﬁve further
selections of 2,000. An ordering is persistent only if it holds in all 26 conditions. This refers
to the order of the two measured values, not to the direction in which a vector points. The
dimension alternatives use the same conditions.
Another check changes the common point from which the arrows are drawn. For each model,
I calculate the mean of the balanced 52,000 unit vectors used in the input-text experiment,
subtract it from every vector, and scale each resulting vector back to length one. This is
global centring followed by normalisation . The new origin is the average position in this set.
I then recalculate spread and dimension to ask whether comparisons between areas depend
on that choice. Supplement S6 distinguishes this change to the vectors from the internal
centring already used in the PR formula.

The results fall into three classes. In unanimous agreement, all ten models persistently give
the same area the larger value. In opposition, at least two ﬁxed models persistently give
opposite orderings. Otherwise the comparison is unresolved. For example, some models may
change their ordering with other articles and no ﬁxed pair may maintain opposite answers.
Unresolved does not mean equal. Nor does a tie in one model necessarily leave the whole
comparison unresolved: two other models can still maintain opposite orderings.
I also ask whether the diﬀerences are large enough to matter numerically. Besides any nonzero
diﬀerence, I require minimum diﬀerences of 1, 5 and 10% of the two areas’ average value. A
model’s ordering counts at a given cutoﬀ only if its diﬀerence exceeds that cutoﬀ in every
selection. The three classiﬁcation rules are then applied again. These percentages describe
the size of a measured diﬀerence, distinct from the percentage of the 325 pairs assigned to a
class. They are sensitivity checks, not universal thresholds of scientiﬁc importance.
A separate diagnostic, the selection alert, marks a result that changes more than a recorded
tolerance allows when we choose other articles or use another tested size. It identiﬁes a result
worth examining for sensitivity to article choice; it is neither a source-record error nor one
of the three area-pair classes above. Each check has its own rule and count, retained in the
supplement.
Selections overlap because they come from the same ﬁxed collection. Their ranges describe
changes among the available papers, rather than conﬁdence intervals for all scientiﬁc liter-
ature. A central 95% selection range leaves out the lowest 2.5% and highest 2.5% of the
recorded results. Comparisons also reuse models and areas, so I describe their patterns
without treating them as independent observations in signiﬁcance tests.
The work developed in stages. The initial structure and neighbour comparisons came before
the expanded text experiment and the specialty searches. The geometric analysis was a
later exploration, and the summary of 325 area pairs followed its ﬁrst examples. Rules were
recorded before the corresponding new calculations, with earlier results already known; some
controls were added along the way. Supplement S2 documents this sequence. The full study
was not registered in advance with an external service, so these later checks should not be
read as predictions made before any results were seen.

4 Results
4.1 The broad arrangement is shared more than the detail
Models share a similar arrangement of area centres, and choosing other papers changes that
conclusion little. Each mean combines the 45 ways of pairing the ten models. For the original
article selection, corrected CKA averages 0.939 for area centres and 0.911 for specialty centres.
Across ﬁfty selections, the averages are 0.940 and 0.911, with central 95% selection ranges
of 0.937–0.943 and 0.910–0.912 (Figure 1A). Randomly redistributed groups average 0.627
and 0.636. None of their selection means reaches the lowest observed selection mean. Thus,
the labelled groups produce more shared structure than these random references.
The arrangement also changes little when centres use more papers. At 128, 256 and 512
papers, area-centre means are 0.938, 0.940 and 0.941; the same 183 eligible specialties give
0.913, 0.914 and 0.915. Leaving out each area in turn gives 0.933–0.946 in the original
comparison, so no single area accounts for the high agreement. When only one specialty
represents each area, the mean is 0.904. In this restricted comparison, changing which
specialty represents an area matters more than changing its selected papers (Table S18).
Agreement is lower when we compare the individual papers inside a group. Mean CKA stays
around 0.63 within areas and 0.64 within the same 183 specialties as the count rises from
128 to 512 (Figure 1B). That stable average hides individual comparisons that change more.
Of the 8,235 combinations of a specialty and a model pair, 2,628 trigger the selection alert
(31.9%): their agreement changes more than the recorded tolerance allows across article
choices or tested sizes. A stable mean therefore does not make every specialty result stable.
The contrast with centres also concerns diﬀerent objects—group summaries versus individual
papers—so it does not isolate the eﬀect of scale alone.
The alternative structure measures broadly agree about which model pairs are more similar.
Within each of the original 130 area–period groups, I order model pairs from less to more
agreement and compare those orderings. Their mean rank correlations with corrected CKA
are 0.891 for Procrustes and 0.881 for distance ranks; values nearer one mean more similar
orderings. Agreement is not complete. The original article-selection check also retains 50
alerts among 5,850 comparisons, corresponding to 45 model pairs in each of the 130 groups
(Table S4). This is a diﬀerent check from the specialty alerts above, with its own sizes and
tolerances.

4.2 Changing the model changes which papers appear nearby
When every paper in the same area and period is available as a candidate, two models
share an average of 30.3% of the 25 nearest neighbours: about eight papers out of 25. The
average gives equal weight to each area–period group and model pair. Holding every group’s
candidate count at 2,048 gives 31.9% agreement. The count is shared across groups; the
actual papers are shared across models within each group, not across disciplines.
The general-collection search asks a broader question. It takes 100 starting papers from each
of the 130 groups, giving 13,000 in total, and searches among the 400,000 general records.
Agreement is 17.5%. This search both admits other disciplines and periods and oﬀers many
more candidates. Because both change together, the comparison cannot tell us how much of
the lower agreement comes from each change.

Filtering source ﬂags and duplicates changes little of this picture. To make the before-and-
after comparison fair, both averages use only the starting papers that survive the ﬁlter.
Their initial agreement is 30.58%, slightly diﬀerent from the 30.3% for all starting papers
above, and rises to 31.43% after ﬁltering candidates. The gain is 0.85 percentage points.
Both averages still mean roughly eight shared papers out of 25. Keeping candidate counts
ﬁxed reduces the gain to 0.28 points with 1,024 candidates and 0.19 with 2,048. The issues
removed by this ﬁlter explain little of the overall disagreement, although some areas change
more than others (Table S19).
The matched specialty searches isolate a change in disciplinary scope more closely: both
searches have 256 candidates, the same starting papers and matched dates (Figure 2). Search-
ing within the specialty makes models share fewer of their 25 neighbours in 127 of 217 spe-
cialties (58.5%), but more in the other 90 (41.5%). The average change from the parent-area
search is −1.35 percentage points. With lists of 50 neighbours, the split is almost even: 109
specialties decrease and 108 increase. Narrowing the search therefore does not have the same
eﬀect everywhere. These results describe how much the models’ lists overlap; they do not
assess how useful the listed papers are.
Pairs of models that agree more on overall structure also tend to share more neighbours.
Ordering the 45 model pairs by each type of agreement gives a rank correlation of 0.920.
This association does not make the two checks interchangeable: a pair can preserve broad
relationships while replacing many individual neighbours. The model pairs also share models

4.4 A comparison between two areas can reverse under another
model
The ﬁnal analysis asks whether models support the same description of an area. It reuses the
input-text experiment’s 52,000 papers and their title-plus-abstract vectors. For example, do
the arrows representing one area’s papers point further apart than those of another? Before
comparing areas, I check whether each area’s measured value changes greatly when other
papers are chosen.
Ten models and 26 areas give 260 cases to check. Angular spread has no selection alerts
in either the half-sample or equal-size check. PR has four alerts (1.5%) with twenty half
samples of 1,000 papers per area, and none with ﬁve further selections of 2,000. These
checks use diﬀerent sizes and repetition counts. Passing the second does not remove the
sensitivity detected by the ﬁrst.
Repeatable values within a model do not ensure agreement between models. Among the
325 area pairs, 80.6% have persistent opposite answers from at least two models for angular
spread, and 68.0% for PR (Figure 4). For example, one ﬁxed model gives area A a larger
angular spread than B in every article selection, while another gives B the larger spread in
every selection. The other models need not all disagree with one another. “Larger” refers to
the measured property, not to a higher position in a drawing or better research.

Unanimous agreement is less common: all ten models persistently give the same area the
larger value in 12.9% of pairs for angular spread and 16.6% for PR. The remaining 6.5%
and 15.4% do not meet either the unanimous-agreement or opposition rule. They remain
unresolved under those rules.
Many reversals concern small diﬀerences between areas. Requiring each persistent ordering
to exceed a 5% diﬀerence, relative to the two measured values’ average, reduces opposition to

the shares fall to 5.8% and 38.8%. The ﬁrst percentage in each comparison is a requirement
on the diﬀerence’s size; the resulting percentage tells us how many pairs still meet it. A
reversal over a tiny diﬀerence and one over a larger diﬀerence should not carry the same
interpretation.
The alternative formulas often retain the disagreement, but not in every case. I count an
opposition as retained only when at least one of the original opposing model pairs keeps
the same area orderings. Alternative angle summaries retain 85.9% of the original angular
oppositions by this rule (225/262). Across the same 26 article conditions, entropy-based
dimension retains 94.6% of the original PR oppositions and D80 retains 86.0%; 86.0% survive
both alternatives (190/221). These denominators are the original opposed pairs, not all area
pairs. Because D80 returns whole numbers, it can give ties that contribute no persistent
ordering.
The four word-level models do not account for all opposition either. Keeping only the six
models trained for similarity still leaves opposite answers in 71.1% of all 325 pairs for angular
spread and 49.2% for PR (Tables S14–S15).

mean position of the balanced 52,000 papers (Figure 4). After subtracting that mean and
restoring unit vector length, opposition falls from 80.6% to 46.5% of all 325 pairs: from 262
to 151. But the new total does not tell us which original disagreements remain. Only 48.9%
of the original oppositions retain at least one of the same opposing model pairs with the
same area orderings (128/262). Table S20 separates these from new oppositions or those
supported by diﬀerent model pairs. With the 5% minimum-diﬀerence rule, only 11 opposed
pairs remain instead of 96.

orderings (204/221). An angular comparison that repeats with other papers can therefore
still depend strongly on the origin from which angles are measured.

5 Discussion
5.1 What should be checked before using a map
A high overall agreement score leaves some practical questions open. The models can share
the broad arrangement of areas while directing a reader towards diﬀerent papers or giving
opposite comparisons between areas. Suppose one model gives area A a larger angular spread
than B, and the result repeats with other articles. That shows stability to article selection
within this model. If another model persistently reverses the order, more selections with the
ﬁrst model do not resolve the disagreement. The claim needs to be checked by changing the
model as well as the articles.
The concern follows earlier work on method-dependent science maps ( Gläser et al. , 2017;
Boyack and Klavans, 2020) and complements assessments against external classiﬁcations and
relationships ( González-Márquez et al. , 2024; Constantino et al. , 2025). Combining struc-
tural and neighbour comparisons also has precedent ( Caspari et al. , 2024). The contribution
here is the controlled comparison across levels, text choices and repeated area comparisons,
including cases where a result persists within models but reverses between them.
Interpretation also depends on what the measure describes. Larger angles mean that vectors
point further apart; a larger eﬀective linear dimension means that their variation is less
concentrated in a few directions. Those are descriptions of the representation. Calling an
area more thematically diverse or interdisciplinary would require a further link between those
numerical properties and the research itself. Related work on embedding geometry cautions
against assigning a broad meaning to a convenient measure ( Rudman et al., 2022). The failed
fragmentation check illustrates why: even a geometric diﬀerence that repeats can support
the wrong explanation.
5.2 What the additional checks change
The additional checks narrow the explanations for the main ﬁndings. The shared arrange-
ment of area centres is not tied to one set of papers, to choosing 128 rather than 256 or 512,
or to including one particular area. The low neighbour overlap is also not largely removed
by the recorded quality ﬁlter: after excluding ﬂagged and duplicate records, two models still
share about eight papers in a list of 25. These checks strengthen those observations within
the tested collection, without establishing the accuracy of the shared relationships.
For input text, the repeated selections preserve an average result that hides diﬀerent model
behaviours. Giving titles alone changes fewer neighbours than switching models for some
models, and more for others. Replacing mean pooling with the ﬁrst or last contextual output
in the four word-level models also reverses the overall ordering. The practical consequence
19
Shape of Science Robustness Manuscript draft
is to specify both what text a model reads and how its outputs become a paper vector. A
statement that text and model choice matter equally would go beyond these results.
For angular spread, the common reference point is another consequential choice. Moving
it to the mean of the balanced article set and rescaling the vectors changes many answers
about which area has the larger spread. Repeating article selection does not remove this
dependence or establish one representation as correct. PR changes less, although alternative
dimension formulas still fail to retain every comparison. A claim about spread therefore
needs to identify the representation, the measure and the size of the diﬀerence.
The discipline checks examine one concrete diﬀerence: models agree less about the relation-
ships among Medicine papers than among Energy papers under the main rule (Supplement
S5). This does not mean that Medicine is less coherent, and it does not always have the
lowest neighbour overlap. The structural diﬀerence remains when the contributions of spe-
cialties within each area are balanced, and when the two biomedical models, BioBERT and
PubMedBERT, are excluded. Neither tested explanation accounts for the whole diﬀerence.
Models with related training also sometimes give similar results, but these apparent families
change with the rule used to form paper vectors. With ten models that diﬀer simultaneously
in training texts, internal design and the task used to train them, these associations cannot
identify one of those features as the cause.
The time comparison applies the same current models to papers from older and newer pub-
lication periods. Structural agreement increases slightly on average. Neighbour agreement
falls by 0.87 percentage points when each group oﬀers all its available papers, but rises by
0.91 points when every group oﬀers 2,048 candidates. The apparent trend therefore depends
on whether older and newer groups are allowed diﬀerent search sizes. It describes agreement
among present-day representations of those papers; it does not, by itself, show that science
has historically converged.
5.3 What remains open
The collection requires English-labelled records with usable abstracts. Its reinforcement of
smaller area–period groups helps comparison but changes proportions. Source errors remain,
and cleaning is not a certiﬁcation of every record. Repeated selections cannot repair missing
coverage or make these results representative of all scientiﬁc work.
The ten models also set a boundary. Four were not trained speciﬁcally for document simi-
larity. The six-model check shows that disagreement is not conﬁned to those four, but does
not make this set representative of all embedding models. To decide which area ordering
or article relationship is better supported, we need evidence outside these vectors, such as
independent assessments of the papers.
Several extensions and controls also followed earlier results. Recording their rules before
the new calculations makes the decisions traceable, but does not turn them into predictions
20
Shape of Science Robustness Manuscript draft
made before the study began. A useful next step would test a speciﬁc map-based claim
against independent judgments in a research area. Adding models alone would not settle
what their shared or conﬂicting geometry means.
6 Conclusion
Changing the embedding model can preserve the broad arrangement of science while chang-
ing which papers appear nearby and which area has more widely spread representations.
Giving a model titles alone can also produce large changes, with eﬀects that depend on the
model and the rule used to form paper vectors. Choosing other articles tests one source
of dependence; changing models or the reference point tests others. Before using a map to
support a particular relationship, we should check that relationship under the choices that
can aﬀect it and show how large the changes are. The tested models’ agreement tells us
what their representations share. Deciding whether that relationship describes the research
well still needs independent evidence.
