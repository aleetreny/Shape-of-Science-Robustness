How much does the map of science depend on
the embedding model?
Alejandro Treny Ortega
Independent researcher
Manuscript draft • 20 September 2026
Abstract
Science maps use relationships between papers to describe how research is organised.
When these relationships come from text embeddings, a model turns each paper into
a list of numbers. Would another model give the same picture? I compare ten models
using 500,000 OpenAlex publications from 2000–2024 across 26 disciplines, including
Medicine and Physics. Representing disciplines by their papers’ average positions pro-
duces similar arrangements across models. This holds when other papers are selected
to calculate those averages. Yet within a discipline and period, two models share, on
average, only eight of the 25 papers closest to a given paper. Giving a model each
paper’s title alone, instead of its title and abstract, also changes much of that list.
The eﬀect varies between models. One model can place a discipline’s papers closer to-
gether than another’s, while another model reverses that order. This comparison uses
angles between paper positions. Moving their reference point can change the answer
even when selecting other papers does not. These checks show which relationships are
shared across representations and which depend on the model, input text or reference
point. Agreement about the broad arrangement can coexist with diﬀerent answers
about nearby papers and comparisons between disciplines.
Keywords: science mapping; document embeddings; robustness; neighbourhood agreement;
geometric comparisons.
1 Introduction
A science map can help a reader ﬁnd related papers or compare research areas. Its usefulness
depends on the relationships it shows. If two models place diﬀerent papers close to the same
article, the reader may follow diﬀerent routes through the literature. If they disagree about
how widely an area’s papers are spread, they may also support diﬀerent descriptions of
that area. I ask how much of this changes when the papers stay the same and only their
representation changes.
Text embeddings provide one way to build these maps. A model reads a paper’s text and
turns it into a list of numbers, called a vector. Those numbers give the paper a position

in a space where distances can be calculated. Papers placed close together are treated as
related. This approach has been used to map biomedical literature and the structure of
physics ( González-Márquez et al. , 2024; Constantino et al. , 2025). But the position comes
from the model. It is not a relationship observed independently of that model.
This concern has a history in science mapping. Gläser et al. (2017) asked how much a
reconstructed thematic structure reﬂects scientiﬁc knowledge and how much it reﬂects the
method used to recover it. Boyack and Klavans (2020) found that methods could obtain
similar overall accuracy while assigning diﬀerent papers to clusters. An overall score can
therefore leave an important question unanswered: would another method support the par-
ticular comparison a reader wants to make?
I address that question using ten models and a ﬁxed collection of 500,000 publications. First,
I compare the broad arrangement of research areas and the articles placed near each other.
I then ask whether changing the text supplied to a model matters as much as changing
the model itself. A later, exploratory analysis examines speciﬁc comparisons between areas:
does each model identify the same area as having more widely spread paper representations?
Repeating these comparisons with other articles helps distinguish dependence on the selected
papers from dependence on the model.
2 Background and research questions
2.1 What earlier comparisons tell us
Previous studies assess scientiﬁc embeddings against several useful criteria. Constantino
et al. (2025) compare them with disciplinary classiﬁcations in physics; González-Márquez
et al. (2024) examine related biomedical papers and their labels. Such checks ask whether
a representation agrees with information about the literature. The present study asks a
complementary question: which relationships remain when the representation changes?
The model is only one choice involved. A title and a full abstract contain diﬀerent amounts of
information. Models also read diﬀerent lengths of text and combine their outputs in diﬀerent
ways. Singh and Singh (2022) show that scholarly search can be sensitive to the wording of
short queries. This makes the text supplied to a model part of the comparison, rather than
a detail to leave unspeciﬁed.
Comparing both overall structure and nearby papers is not new. Caspari et al. (2024)
combine geometric and neighbourhood comparisons, while Imel and Hafen (2025) examine
properties of scientiﬁc embedding spaces alongside citation relationships. I build on these ap-
proaches by keeping articles matched between models, changing text and processing choices
explicitly, and checking speciﬁc comparisons between research areas with repeated article
selections and alternative measures.

2.2 What agreement can establish
Throughout the article, an article selection means choosing a set of papers from the ﬁxed
collection. Repeating the selection means choosing another set under the same size and date
rules, without adding newly downloaded papers. Agreement asks whether diﬀerent models
give similar results for the same papers. Selection stability asks whether a result repeats
when the selected papers change. A comparison can be stable within each model while the
models give opposite answers.
Neither property establishes that the answer is correct about the research itself. Models can
share an error, and a repeatable pattern can be hard to interpret. In a study of invasion
biology, Held and Velden (2022) show why topic solutions require substantive interpretation
beyond numerical checks. Here, agreement is evidence that a result survives a change of
model, not evidence of thematic truth.
I compare the numerical representations before they are reduced to a drawing on a page.
Each vector has hundreds of coordinates; a two-dimensional plot would add another trans-
formation that could change apparent distances. In this article, the shape of a map refers
to relationships in that original space.
2.3 Research questions
RQ1. When the model changes, what remains of the arrangement of research areas and of
the papers placed close together?
RQ2. How much does changing the input text, or the rule used to form one paper vector,
matter compared with changing the model?
RQ3. Do models agree about which research area has more widely spread paper repre-
sentations, or variation across more directions? Do these comparisons repeat with other
articles?
The third question was added after the initial comparisons and is exploratory. All three
concern a ﬁxed collection and a ﬁxed set of models. They do not seek a winning model or
estimate the structure of all scientiﬁc literature.

3 Data and methods
3.1 One collection, several questions
The starting collection contains 500,000 distinct OpenAlex work IDs, retrieved and cleaned
by 15 September 2026 ( Priem et al. , 2022). Records were labelled as English articles, reviews
or conference papers published in 2000–2024. Each required a title, a reconstructed abstract
of at least 50 words and a primary research ﬁeld. Retractions, paratext and clear content
failures were excluded. Less certain cases and possible duplicate versions were kept with
ﬂags, rather than treated as resolved. Supplement S1 gives the rules and counts.
OpenAlex assigns papers to broad Fields and narrower Subﬁelds, using information from
text, citations and journals ( OpenAlex, 2026). I call these areas and specialties. For example,
Medicine is an area; Cardiology and Cardiovascular Medicine is one of its specialties. The
26 areas provide the main units of comparison. These database labels organise the analysis;
they are not independently veriﬁed descriptions of every paper.
The collection has two parts. Seeded random API requests supplied 400,000 eligible records.
Another 100,000 strengthened coverage where few papers were available within an area and
ﬁve-year period. The ﬁve periods are 2000–04, 2005–09, 2010–14, 2015–19 and 2020–24,
giving 130 area–period groups with at least 2,165 papers each. Global searches use the
400,000-record general collection. Area comparisons use both parts. Neither the reinforced
collection nor an average giving every area equal weight estimates world publication propor-
tions.
Not every question requires processing all 500,000 papers again. To compare titles, abstracts
and both together, I use the same 52,000 papers: 2,000 per area, with 400 from each period.
This includes the 26,000 papers used in an earlier, smaller trial of that same comparison. The
later analysis of spread within areas reuses these 52,000 papers. A separate set of 52,000
serves a diﬀerent purpose: checking what happens when every model reads the same passage
of text. Table 1 keeps these uses apart. In every model comparison, both models receive the
same article IDs.

3.2 How each model represents a paper
The ten models in Table 2 cover scientiﬁc documents, general sentence similarity, and scien-
tiﬁc or biomedical language. They were selected for their use in scholarly representation and
their diﬀerent training approaches. They are a ﬁxed comparison set, not a random sample
of models or a ranking of the newest systems. Model versions, text formats and software
settings were ﬁxed before their representations were calculated.
The main input is the title followed by the abstract, in each model’s usual format. Models
read small pieces of text called tokens, which may be whole words or parts of words. Their
input limits therefore diﬀer from word counts. MiniLM reads at most 256 tokens and MPNet
384 in their standard settings. These limits cut oﬀ part of the input for 49.58% and 20.16% of
papers, respectively. I retain those settings and test MiniLM at 512 tokens separately. The
common-passage check described above lets every model read the same content, although
each divides it into tokens in its own way.
Some models produce a vector for each token, so a further rule must turn those outputs into
one vector for the paper. This step is called pooling. For BERT, SciBERT, BioBERT and
PubMedBERT, the main rule averages the last-layer outputs at all positions that contain
text or special symbols, excluding padding. These four models were not trained speciﬁcally
to compare whole documents. Two checks instead use the ﬁrst-position output, called CLS,
or the last non-padding output, called SEP. CLS is taken before the model’s additional pooler
transformation. The mean rule was speciﬁed before the primary comparisons and retained
after early checks; it was not chosen to maximise agreement. The other six models use their
documented rules. Supplement S1 provides exact settings.
3.3 Following the map from areas to individual papers
Before comparing representations, each article vector is scaled to length one. This makes
comparisons depend on direction rather than vector length. To summarise an area, I average
its article vectors and scale that centre to length one as well. The arrangement of these
centres describes broad relationships between areas. Comparisons of the individual vectors
inside each area retain the detail hidden by that average.
The original centre comparison uses 256 articles per centre: 26 areas, 217 specialties, or one
specialty from each area. I then repeat article selection ﬁfty times, keeping each group’s
period counts. A size check uses 128, 256 and 512 papers, with the same 26 areas and
183 eligible specialties. Each selection has a random-group reference made from exactly
the same articles and date counts. Further checks omit each area and separate the choice of
specialty from the choice of its papers. These centre comparisons are distinct from comparing
individual vectors within groups (Supplement S9).

For an individual paper, its neighbours are the papers placed closest to it. The starting paper

3.4 Measuring shared structure and spread within an area
To compare overall structure, I ask whether the same papers have similar patterns of relation-
ships in two representations. The main measure is corrected linear centred kernel alignment,
abbreviated CKA ( Kornblith et al. , 2019; Murphy et al. , 2024). A value near one, such as
0.9, indicates strong similarity between these patterns on this measure: the models organise
the same objects in similar ways. It is an agreement score, not a percentage of correctly
represented science. The correction reduces a known inﬂation caused by ﬁnite samples; it
does not remove all statistical bias. Two further checks allow a rotation and rescaling of the
representations (Procrustes alignment), or compare the ranking of distances between papers.
They respond to diﬀerent aspects of the representations ( Williams et al. , 2021; Williams,
2024). Supplement S2 gives the formulas.
I also describe two properties within each area. Angular spread measures how far apart article
vectors point: it summarises the angles between distinct vectors, using the median-distance
convention in Supplement S6. Larger angles mean a wider spread of directions. Eﬀective
linear dimension , measured by the participation ratio (PR), asks how widely variation is
shared among directions. For intuition, a cloud concentrated along one line varies mainly in
one direction; a cloud spread across a plane varies in more. The measure extends that idea
to the full vector space. Neither property counts research topics or establishes how diverse
an area is.
Alternative angle summaries and measures of variation test dependence on the chosen for-
mula. For dimension, one alternative measures how evenly variation is distributed (entropy-
based dimension); another counts the directions needed to account for 80% of it (D80).
These alternatives use the same underlying variation, so they do not independently validate
a thematic interpretation. I also tested whether links between nearby papers could iden-
tify separate groups, but checks with artiﬁcial examples prevented treating that measure as
general fragmentation. Deﬁnitions and controls appear in Supplement S6.

3.5 Checking whether a comparison repeats
With 26 areas, there are 325 ways to choose two diﬀerent areas, counting each comparison
once. For each geometric property, I ask which area has the larger value under each model.
I repeat the calculation using twenty half samples of 1,000 papers per area and ﬁve further
selections of 2,000. A direction is called persistent only if it holds in all these selections
and in the main set of 2,000: 26 conditions in total. The two dimension alternatives use
the same conditions. A separate check changes the point from which vector directions are
measured. For each model, I calculate the mean of the balanced 52,000 unit vectors, subtract
this common mean from every vector and restore each vector to length one. This operation,
called global centring followed by normalisation, moves the reference point to the collection’s
mean position. It changes the representation before measuring spread and dimension; it is
distinct from the covariance centring within PR.
This gives three outcomes: all ten models consistently identify the same area as having
the larger value; at least two ﬁxed models consistently give opposite answers; or neither
condition holds. I call the third outcome unresolved. For example, some models may change
their ordering when other papers are selected, without any ﬁxed pair maintaining opposite
answers. A tied or changing ordering does not count as a persistent direction. If neither
complete agreement nor ﬁxed opposition remains, the pair is unresolved; this does not mean
the areas are equal. Besides any nonzero diﬀerence, I examine minimum diﬀerences of 1, 5
and 10% of the two values’ average. A diﬀerence below the required minimum also remains
unresolved. These cutoﬀs show whether very small diﬀerences drive a result.
Two selections can contain many of the same papers because both come from the ﬁxed
collection. The range of their results therefore describes what changes when we choose other
available papers; it is not a conﬁdence interval for all scientiﬁc literature. A selection alert
marks a result whose variation across article choices, or shift between tested sizes, exceeds a
recorded tolerance. It identiﬁes sensitivity to the selected articles, not a source-record error.
The supplement gives each check’s rules and counts. Comparisons also reuse models and
areas, so their results are not treated as independent observations in signiﬁcance tests.
The work developed in stages. The ﬁrst structure and neighbour comparisons preceded the
expanded text experiment and the specialty searches. The geometric analysis was a later
exploration; the 325-comparison summary followed its initial examples. Rules were recorded
before the corresponding new calculations, with earlier results already known. Some controls
were added during this process. Supplement S2 records the sequence; the study was not
externally preregistered as a whole.

4.2 Changing the model changes which papers appear nearby
Within the same area and period, two models retain an average of 30.3% of the 25 nearest
neighbours: about eight papers out of 25. This average gives equal weight to each group
and model pair. Limiting every area–period group to 2,048 candidates raises agreement to
31.9%. The count is the same across groups; the article IDs are shared across models within
each group, not across diﬀerent disciplines. A separate search takes 100 starting papers
from each of the 130 groups, giving 13,000 in total, and ﬁnds their neighbours among the
400,000 general records. Here, agreement is 17.5%. That search both admits papers from
other disciplines and periods and oﬀers many more candidates. Either change could aﬀect
agreement, so their eﬀects cannot be separated by this comparison.
I then remove records carrying the tested source ﬂags and keep one representative of each de-
tected duplicate group. For the same retained starting papers, neighbour agreement changes
from 30.58% to 31.43%: a gain of 0.85 percentage points. Both averages still amount to
roughly eight shared papers in a list of 25. Holding candidate counts ﬁxed makes the change
smaller: 0.28 points with 1,024 candidates and 0.19 with 2,048. The issues removed by this
ﬁlter therefore explain little of the overall disagreement, although some areas change more
than others (Table S19).
The paired searches let us change the disciplinary scope while keeping candidate counts and
dates matched (Figure 2). Searching only within a specialty makes models share fewer of
their 25 nearest neighbours in 127 of 217 specialties (58.5%), but more in the other 90 (41.5%).
The average change relative to searching the parent area is −1.35 percentage points. With
lists of 50 neighbours, the split is almost even: 109 specialties decrease and 108 increase.
Restricting the search therefore does not have the same eﬀect everywhere; these changes
concern overlap between models’ lists, rather than how useful the listed papers are.

the input context. With these alternatives, title-only input changes fewer neighbours than

for SEP at 25 neighbours. Both comparisons are recalculated with the chosen rule; the other
six models retain their usual settings. Thus, the average ordering reverses when we change
this practical way of using the four models.
This three-input experiment began with 26,000 papers and then added another 26,000 under
the same design; the ﬁrst set is included in the ﬁnal 52,000. The same structural selection
check was applied at both sizes, choosing other papers 100 times. Alerts fall from 72 of
520 model–area–input comparisons (13.8%) to ﬁve of 520 (1.0%). Fewer results therefore
exceed the recorded tolerance, although this does not establish stability for every neighbour
comparison. Supplement S3 keeps the earlier twenty-selection check separate. The common-
passage and MiniLM 512-token checks address a diﬀerent question, input length; they do
not reveal a coding error in MiniLM’s original limit.
4.4 A comparison between two areas can reverse under another
model
The geometric analysis reuses the 52,000-paper text sample and its title-plus-abstract rep-
resentations. It asks whether, for example, one area’s vectors point further apart than
another’s. I ﬁrst checked how much each measure changes when diﬀerent articles are chosen.
Across ten models and 26 areas, angular spread has no alerts in the half-sample or equal-size
checks. PR has four alerts among 260 cases (1.5%) when using twenty half samples of 1,000
papers per area. None appears in ﬁve further selections of 2,000 papers per area. These
checks use diﬀerent sizes and repetition counts; passing the second does not erase sensitivity
found in the ﬁrst.
Of the 325 area pairs, 80.6% have persistent opposite answers from at least two models
for angular spread; the share is 68.0% for PR (Figure 4). For example, one ﬁxed model
consistently gives area A a larger angular spread than B, while another consistently gives B
the larger spread. “Larger” refers to the measured value, not a higher position in a drawing or
better research. Other models need not disagree with one another. All ten consistently agree

5 Discussion
5.1 What should be checked before using a map
The models share a broad arrangement, yet can disagree about nearby papers or comparisons
between areas. Suppose one model gives area A a larger angular spread than B, and this
repeats with other papers. That establishes stability to article selection within that model.
If another model consistently reverses the order, repeating the ﬁrst model’s selections cannot
settle the disagreement. A map-based claim therefore needs to be checked by changing the
model as well as the articles.
The concern follows earlier work on method-dependent science maps ( Gläser et al. , 2017;
Boyack and Klavans, 2020) and complements assessments against external classiﬁcations and
relationships ( González-Márquez et al. , 2024; Constantino et al. , 2025). Combining struc-
tural and neighbour comparisons also has precedent ( Caspari et al. , 2024). The contribution
16
Shape of Science Robustness Manuscript draft
here is the controlled comparison across levels, text choices and repeated area comparisons,
including cases where a result persists within models but reverses between them.
Interpretation still depends on what the measure describes. A larger angle means that
vectors point further apart. A larger eﬀective linear dimension means that variation is less
concentrated in a few directions. Neither, by itself, shows greater thematic diversity or
interdisciplinarity. Related work on embedding geometry also cautions against assigning a
broad meaning to a convenient measure ( Rudman et al. , 2022). The failed fragmentation
check makes this concrete: a repeatable diﬀerence can still support the wrong explanation.
5.2 What the additional checks change
The checks help separate ﬁndings that repeat from choices that still aﬀect them. Recalculat-
ing area averages with other papers, with 128, 256 or 512 papers, or leaving out one area at
a time preserves high agreement about the broad arrangement. Removing the ﬂagged and
duplicate records barely changes overall neighbour overlap: the two models still share about
eight papers in a list of 25.
For input text, repeating article selection preserves the average comparison, but that average
combines diﬀerent model behaviours. Giving only titles changes fewer neighbours than
switching models for some models, and more for others. Replacing the averaging rule with
CLS or SEP for the four BERT models also reverses the overall ordering. The practical
implication is to specify both what text a model reads and how its outputs become a paper
vector; the average cannot stand for every conﬁguration.
For angular spread, the reference point is another consequential choice. The same papers can
give a diﬀerent answer about which area is more widely spread when angles are measured
from the collection’s mean position and vectors are rescaled. Repeating article selection does
not remove that dependence. PR changes less under this transformation, but its alternative
formulas do not retain every comparison either. A claim that one area is more spread out
should therefore identify the representation and measure used, rather than leave these choices
implicit.
The discipline checks ask why Medicine and Energy diﬀer (Supplement S5). With the main
rule, models agree less on relationships among Medicine papers than among Energy papers,
although Medicine does not always have the lowest neighbour overlap. The diﬀerence re-
mains after balancing the representation of specialties within each area and after excluding
BioBERT and PubMedBERT. These checks therefore do not identify a single explanation.
Models with related training also sometimes give similar results, but these apparent families
change when the rule for forming paper vectors changes. With only ten models that diﬀer in
several ways at once, the comparisons cannot isolate training data, architecture or training
objective as the cause.

The time comparison uses the same current models on papers from older and newer publica-
tion periods. Structural agreement increases slightly on average. Neighbour agreement falls
by 0.87 percentage points when each group oﬀers all its available papers, but rises by 0.91
points when every group oﬀers 2,048 candidates. Older and newer groups contain diﬀerent
numbers of papers; ﬁxing that number reverses the direction of the result. This shows why
candidate counts must be controlled before interpreting a time trend. It does not establish
that science itself has historically converged.
5.3 What remains open
The collection requires English-labelled records with usable abstracts. Its reinforcement of
smaller area–period groups helps comparison but changes proportions. Source errors remain,
and cleaning is not a certiﬁcation of every record. Repeated selections cannot repair missing
coverage or make these results representative of all scientiﬁc work.
The ten models also set a boundary. Four were not trained speciﬁcally for document simi-
larity. The six-model check helps show that disagreement is not conﬁned to those four, but
does not make the panel representative of all encoders. Deciding which direction is better
supported requires evidence outside the embeddings, such as independently assessed article
relationships.
Finally, several extensions and controls followed earlier results. Recording their rules before
new calculations improves traceability without turning the whole study into a conﬁrma-
tory design. A useful next step would test a deﬁned map-based claim against independent
judgments in a research area. Adding models alone would not settle what their shared or
conﬂicting geometry means.
6 Conclusion
Changing the embedding model can preserve the broad arrangement of science while changing
which papers appear nearby and which area has more widely spread representations. Giving
a model only titles can also produce large changes, whose size depends on the model and
the rule for forming paper vectors. Repeating the analysis with other papers is useful, but
does not show that another model or reference point would support the same answer. Before
using a map to describe a relationship, check that relationship under those choices and report
how large the changes are. These comparisons identify what the tested representations share;
judging whether the relationship describes the research well still needs independent evidence.
